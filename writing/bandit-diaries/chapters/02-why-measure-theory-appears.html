Why Measure Theory Appears

Why probability needs sigma-algebras, measures, and integration once simple sums stop being enough.

Shirish Chinchanikar

This chapter is about measure theory.

This is where we learn about measures, sigma-algebras, random variables, integration, and expectation. I do not want to get into too much detail here, so I will just write about why I feel measure theory is important.

Where sigma-algebras come from

Suppose you are trying to invent a notion of length on subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\). You would want the lengths of disjoint sets to add up, and translating a set should not change its length.

The problem is that there are some crazy subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\) for which you cannot define length consistently. One such construction comes from dividing \(\mathbb{R}\) into equivalence classes of \(\mathbb{Q}\) in \(\mathbb{R}\), where two numbers are considered equivalent if their difference is rational, and then choosing one representative from each class.

Because of sets like these, we cannot safely define a measure on the entire power set \(\mathcal P(\mathbb{R})\). Instead, we define a sigma-algebra, which is a collection of sets on which a measure can be defined consistently.

A probability measure then satisfies three main properties: probabilities are non-negative, the probability of the entire outcome space is \(1\), and the probability of a countable union of disjoint sets is the sum of their probabilities.

Why integration appears

For a discrete random variable, we can calculate expectation using a sum:

\[ \mathbb E[X] = \sum_x x\,P(X=x). \]

But when the random variable has a genuinely continuous distribution, each individual outcome can have probability zero. So we cannot calculate expectation by simply summing over the probabilities of individual points.

Instead, we define a probability measure over sets and integrate the random variable with respect to that measure:

\[ \mathbb E[X] = \int X\,dP. \]

Sometimes this probability measure has a density \(f\) with respect to the usual notion of length on \(\mathbb{R}\). In that case, \(f\) is the Radon–Nikodym derivative, and the expectation becomes

\[ \mathbb E[X] = \int x f(x)\,dx. \]

Main idea

Measure theory gives us one language for discrete, continuous, and even stranger probability distributions. Without it, probability starts breaking apart into a pile of separate cases.
